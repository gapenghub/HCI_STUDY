Human Factors & Stakeholders

The big idea: before you design anything, understand the human. Human factors = the study of human limitations & capabilities so we can design products that are comfortable, accessible and error-resistant. Users are limited in how much they can process — design around that.

The 3 areas of human factors — they overlap on every interaction

Physicalphysiology · ergonomics

The body's limits: reach, size, strength, speed, stamina. Needs ergonomic design so tasks feel efficient & comfortable.

3 things specialists analyse — Operations (the motor actions: tap, swipe, type), Dimension (size/shape/weight vs the hand), Context (environment: driving, gloves, sunlight).

Accommodate P-T-S disabilities: Permanent (one arm), Temporary (broken wrist), Situational (holding a baby, gesture fatigue from long scrolling).

Watch forMovement time depends on a target's size & distance (Fitts's Law): make key buttons big & near the thumb; edges/corners are easy hits.

Cognitionmental effort

How we understand, think, remember & reason. The mental effort to use a product = cognitive load. Too much load → frustration & errors.

Four cognition levers: Attention (focus, kill clutter), Memory, Learning (build on what users know), Language (plain words, short chunks).

Don't forgetCauses of high load: info overload, visual clutter, hard navigation, complex forms, unclear language.

Perceptionthe senses

How we take input through our senses (sight, sound, touch, taste, smell). In GUIs, almost everything is visual.

Eyes jump around in quick fixations called saccades — putting something on screen doesn't guarantee it's seen. Guide the eye.

Improve itGroup related info together, use proximity, visual clues (arrows/icons), and good spacing/kerning.
Load × 3
I-E-G cognitive load: Intrinsic = task's built-in difficulty · Extraneous = bad design noise (fonts, fluff) · Germane = effort to build mental schemas. Designers cut Extraneous, manage Intrinsic.
7 ± 2
Miller's Law: short-term memory holds about 7 ± 2 chunks. So keep menus, options and list rows to ~5–9. Chunk long pages into sections (the eBay trick).
Metaphors

Interface metaphor

A whole concept mapped from the real world onto the system — the desktop, folders, recycle bin. Maps real-world elements onto system elements.

Iconic metaphor

A single symbol standing for a function — a floppy = save, magnifier = search. Use concrete objects + tooltips; avoid abstract ideas.

Why use them
Familiar → easier to learn, builds on existing knowledge, and acts as a shortcut to complex concepts. Helps recognition over recall.
Stakeholder analysis

A stakeholder is anyone who can affect or is affected by the project. Internal = direct relationship (staff, owners, investors). External = affected from outside (customers, suppliers, government, media). Stakeholder analysis = identify them, group them by power & interest, and decide how to communicate with each.

Power →
High power · Low interest

Context-setters

Powerful but not very engaged — can still hurt the project.

▸ Keep satisfied · e.g. Government

Mendelow 3 steps
1. List stakeholders (name, role, internal/external, why). 2. Plot & group them on the power/interest grid. 3. Decide an engagement/communication strategy for each.
Apply it →

For a comms app (e.g. WhatsApp): Perception = unread badges & ticks must be instantly visible; Cognition = chat list chunked, low load; Physical = send button in the thumb zone. Stakeholders → users = Subjects, the parent company = Players, regulators = Context-setters.

Task Analysis & HTA

What it is: a method for understanding the tasks users actually carry out with an existing system — the sequence, complexity, tools, skills and info needed. It studies what is, not new ideas yet. Done via interviews, contextual inquiry and task-based usability testing.

Norman 1988
Goal = the state the user wants to reach. Task = the activity done to bring about that goal. Task analysis maps the real steps — which often differ from what we assume.
Three approaches

Contextualin the wild

Observe & interview users in their real environment to catch true needs & "hot-button" motivators — e.g. test a bike-share app outside, on the move.

Cognitivethe mind

Focuses on the deeper mental processes — decisions, attention, memory, judgement — to see how users understand & perform a task.

Hierarchical (HTA)break it down

Breaks a complex task into smaller subtasks so each step can be analysed on its own. The one you'll be drawing.

HTA = Goal → Tasks → Subtasks, with a Plan saying the order
0. Track parcel on Shopee 1. Open the Shopee website 1.1 Enter "shopee.com.my" 2. Go to My Purchases 2.1 Find the username on the home page 2.2 Click "My Purchase" 3. Choose parcel & check delivery status Plan 0: do 1 → 2 → 3 in order · Plan 2: do 2.1 then 2.2
the same HTA in textual notation — the test can ask for either form, so know both
How to conduct an HTA
01

Prep & research

Gather user needs, tasks, pain points; observe the system used for real.

02

Define use case

Set the scope — what to map & why. This is the GOAL component.

03

Initial flowchart

Draft all steps: list the tasks, subtasks & plans.

04

Develop diagram

Build it — graphical or textual representation.

05

Review

Check accuracy via a walk-through with stakeholders.

06

Report findings

Turn it into a matrix & recommend fixes.

Analyse with C-D-F
Score each step on Criticality, Difficulty & Frequency (H/M/L). Spots the confusing, high-traffic steps that need redesign — e.g. an unlabelled icon with no feedback.
Apply it →

If asked to draw an HTA for an app (login, post a story, send money), state the Goal, list 3–5 tasks, break one into subtasks, and add a one-line Plan for the order. Mention any assumptions.

Usability & the UEL Model

"Usability: the extent to which a product can be used by specified users to achieve specified goals with effectiveness, efficiency and satisfaction in a specified context of use."— ISO definition. Poor usability cuts productivity and raises cost, time & effort.
Nielsen's 5 usability attributes (2003)

Learnability

How easy is it for a novice to get started & become competent quickly?

Efficiency

Once learned, how fast can users perform tasks (expert performance)?

Memorability

Coming back after a break, how easily do they re-establish proficiency?

Errors

Prevent serious slips (don't put Delete next to Save) & help users recover.

Satisfaction

How pleasant is it to use? Fun, helpful, rewarding — the user's point of view.

Remember as
L · E · M · E · S

Who are you designing for?

Novicefirst-timers

Little/no knowledge of the task or interface. Needs learnability & hand-holding.

Knowledgeable intermittentcasual

Knows the task, uses it on & off — struggles with retention. Needs memorability.

Expert / powerfrequent

Knows task & interface cold. Wants shortcuts & efficiency.

Measure it
Learnability/Memorability/Efficiency → time novice/casual/expert users to do typical tasks. Errors → count minor & catastrophic errors during a task.
Usability Engineering Lifecycle — Bias & Mayhew (2005)

A cyclic model bolted into product development to guide usability design, analysis & testing. It loops — you keep iterating.

①

Users, tasks & environment

Stakeholder analysis, user profiling, task analysis.

②

Goals & competitive analysis

Pick usability goals + metrics + design guidelines; check rivals.

③

Design + prototype

Parallel design → participatory design (card sorting) → first prototype.

④

Formative testing

Evaluate as early as possible to catch issues.

⑤

Final release concept

Hand the agreed design to development to build.

↺ cyclic — findings feed back into earlier stages
Apply it →

"Which usability goals did you focus on?" is exactly the presentation question. For most student apps: Learnability + Efficiency + Error prevention. Always justify why that goal fits that page/user.

Don Norman's Design Principles

The compass. Six principles that reduce errors and make interaction intuitive. They guide decisions throughout a project — and they're the language you use to evaluate any UI.

1 · Visibility

Make the most-needed functions easy to see & find. Don't show everything — prioritise.

ExampleTab bars over hidden hamburger menus; Gmail's obvious "Compose".

2 · Feedback

Send info back about what just happened so users never guess. Audio, visual, tactile, verbal.

Two typesActivation = sensory (button changes colour). Behavioural = "it worked" (order confirmed).

3 · Constraints

Restrict what's possible right now to stop mistakes — e.g. greying out options that aren't valid yet.

ExampleA checkout that forces steps in the correct sequence.

4 · Mapping

A natural relationship between a control and its effect. Up/right = more, down/left = less.

ExampleVolume slider with growing bars; + on the right, − on the left.

5 · Consistency

Same things look & behave the same way — patterns. Nav, colours, button positions repeat.

WhyUsers form one mental model & reuse it across the whole site.

6 · Affordances

An object's look tells you how to use it. A 3-D button says "click me"; a slider says "drag me".

In GUIsCalled perceived affordances — buttons, links, tabs, sliders, spinners.
Pair them up
Remember Norman's six in three pairs: Visibility + Feedback (can I see & do I know it worked?) · Constraints + Mapping (what can I do & does it make sense?) · Consistency + Affordances (is it familiar & obvious?).
Apply it →

This is the case-study goldmine. For any page, name 2–3 principles and point to the actual element: "The login page uses Feedback (red text on wrong password) and Constraints (Login button disabled until both fields are filled)." Concrete element = full marks; just describing the page = 5/10. Questions also chain: pick the usability goal first (Ch 6), then the 2–3 principles that achieve that goal.

Evaluation

What: a systematic process of assessing an interface's usability, effectiveness & experience using qualitative + quantitative data. Why: find usability issues early, improve UX, and validate design decisions before costly redesigns. When: from early concept (paper prototypes) right through to post-launch (analytics & surveys).

Formative vs Summative

Formative

during design · iterative
  • Done while designing/developing
  • Aims to improve the product
  • Catches issues early
  • Mostly qualitative insight

Summative

after build · final
  • Done after development is complete
  • Judges overall effectiveness
  • Measures vs predefined criteria
  • Mostly quantitative measurement
Trick
Formative = forming it (still shaping). Summative = summing up (final verdict).
The DECIDE framework
D
Determine

the overall goals of the evaluation.

E
Explore

the specific, measurable questions to answer.

C
Choose

the evaluation paradigm & techniques.

I
Identify

practical issues (users, venue, kit, budget).

D
Decide

how to handle ethics (consent forms, briefing).

E
Evaluate

analyse & present the data (exec summary, findings, recs).

The 4 evaluation paradigms

Quick & Dirtyanytime

Informal, fast feedback to check ideas match users. Speed over documentation.

Field studiesnatural setting

See what users do naturally & how tech affects them. Finds requirements & opportunities.

Usability testingreal users

Recruit target users; a facilitator runs hands-on tasks under controlled conditions while a researcher observes.

Predictiveno users

Experts predict problems using heuristics — quick & cheap. Heuristic evaluation lives here.

Don't mix these up
Usability testing = real target USERS. Heuristic evaluation = at least 3 EXPERTS, no users needed, judged against Nielsen's heuristics. Experts are impartial & don't tie up internal staff.
Nielsen's 10 Heuristics
1
Visibility of system status

Keep users informed with timely feedback.

2
Match system & real world

Speak the user's language, not jargon.

3
User control & freedom

Clear "emergency exit" — undo/redo.

4
Consistency & standards

Follow platform conventions.

5
Error prevention

Stop problems before they happen.

6
Recognition over recall

Show options; don't make users remember.

7
Flexibility & efficiency

Shortcuts for experts, hidden from novices.

8
Aesthetic & minimalist

No irrelevant info competing for attention.

9
Recover from errors

Plain-language messages + a fix.

10
Help & documentation

Best if not needed — but available when it is.

Apply it →

Likely question: "How many DECIDE stages?" → 6. "Which test uses experts vs users?" → heuristic = experts, usability testing = users. To critique an app, walk it against a few of Nielsen's 10 and name the violation.
